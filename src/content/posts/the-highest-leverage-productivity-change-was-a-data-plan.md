For a long time my learning habit lived at home, in the evening, competing with everything else that also lives at home in the evening. It lost most of that competition. Home is where the chores are, where the people who want your attention are, where the couch is. I'd sit down with good intentions and a technical article open in a tab and get maybe fifteen focused minutes before something pulled me away from it. I didn't have a discipline problem exactly — I had a location problem, and I kept trying to solve a location problem with more willpower, which doesn't work any better here than it does anywhere else.

The window that actually worked, once I noticed it, was the commute. Two enclosed, mostly uninterruptible stretches of time a day, existing whether I used them or not. On the train there's nothing to do but be on the train. In the car there's nothing to do but drive. Neither of those facts changes based on how tired or motivated I am, which is exactly what makes them more reliable than an evening slot that depends on both.

The train leg I filled with audio readouts of technical articles and posts — queue up the reading list, let it read to me, eyes free for watching the platform for my stop. I also let myself use that window for video, but under one explicit rule: only educational, never entertainment. Not because entertainment is bad, but because if I let the line blur even once, the commute quietly reverts to being a video-watching window instead of a learning window, and I'd already lost that fight at home. A rule that's binary and slightly arbitrary — is this a talk or a lecture or a walkthrough, yes or no — is enforceable in a way that "use good judgment" never is when I'm tired and the thumbnail is good.

The driving leg is audio-only by necessity, which turned out to be a feature. Same article queue, same YouTube videos except played for audio, same principle. Two different commute modes, same underlying practice: default the dead time to input instead of silence, and keep the content on one side of a line I don't get to renegotiate in the moment.

None of that is the part worth writing down, though. The part worth writing down is what actually made it work reliably, and it wasn't a new resolution or a better queue of content — it was going from a 1GB mobile data plan to a 25GB one.

That sounds too small to matter and I'd have agreed with you before it happened. But the 1GB plan meant every commute session came with a background tax: a running mental tally of how much data an article or a video was going to cost, whether I was close to the cap, whether this was the day I'd get throttled or billed extra. That tax was small in any single instance and constant across all of them, and constant small friction is exactly the kind of thing that kills a daily habit without ever being the dramatic reason you'd point to if asked why it lapsed. I didn't stop commute-learning because I decided to. I stopped, some days, because loading one more article felt like it cost something, and "feels like it costs something" is enough friction to lose to "I'll just look at my phone instead," which costs nothing.

Bumping the plan to 25GB removed the tally entirely. I stopped thinking about data the same way I'd stopped thinking about, say, whether the train has enough seats — it became a solved background condition instead of a live decision I was re-litigating twice a day. The behavior didn't change because I got more disciplined. It changed because the cost of the desired behavior dropped to zero and the cost of the undesired one — doing nothing, or half-doing something — stayed exactly where it was. Once that was true, the habit that motivation had never reliably produced started happening on its own, most days, without me negotiating with myself about it each morning.

I think this generalizes further than data plans. Self-teaching advice skews heavily toward the psychological — motivation, discipline, growth mindset, the willingness to be bad at something in public, which I've written about and still believe matters. But a fair amount of what actually determines whether a learning habit survives contact with a normal week is logistics: whether the friction to start is at zero or at "let me think about whether this is worth it right now." Willpower is a bad tool for beating friction, because friction is small enough each time that willpower always feels like overkill, and then it just doesn't show up. The fix isn't usually more willpower. It's finding the specific small recurring cost that's quietly taxing the behavior you want, and removing it structurally so you never have to spend willpower on it again.

The train and the car were always available. The data plan was the thing standing between "available" and "actually used."
